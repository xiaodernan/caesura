Short story · The Unfinished Hall · No. 3 “The Empty Bar”

空拍子

The Empty Bar

未竟厅 · 七十三年轮值 · 最后一晚的前一夜

Unfinished Hall · seventy-three years of rotation

约 5 分钟 · 世界谱 与 游戏 同源世界观 · 零 LLM 撰写

about 5 minutes · same world as the codex and the game · written without LLM assistance

未竟厅的轮值是七十三年前开始的。

没有人记得第一年是谁敲了第一个音——只记得那一年谁被分到指挥席。轮到恩希尔那一夜，三百个喉咙已经哑了，但他照常站上了台。

· · ·

他的谱台是空的。谱架上没有谱，只有一根指挥棒，和一把旧椅子——不属于它的木纹，不属于他的指节。

一个没有名字的年轻人坐在前排，他只负责两件事：记节拍，沉默。

· · ·

每一遍轮值，恩希尔会做同一件事——举起指挥棒，停两拍，放下。再举，再停，再放。

年轻人数过很多次。举棒到放下，大约是三拍。有一次，他忍不住问：

· · ·

“你在等谁？”

恩希尔没有放下谱台上的指挥棒。

“我在为空拍留一格位置。”

年轻人不懂：

“一格位置？”

· · ·

恩希尔说：“完整的沉默也是一种节拍。要把它写进去，光有音不够。”

年轻人又问：“那为什么一直要这样？”

恩希尔说：“因为总会有一点。总会有人按那个拍落在里面。”

· · ·

那一夜，没有钟响。

但厅里出现了一扇从没开过的门。门的那一边传来一个很轻的脚步声——不像人，也不像风，更像一段迟了七十三年、终于落下来的音符。

恩希尔没有转头。

他把指挥棒轻轻立回谱台上，对着台面按下了第一拍——一个没有声音的节拍。

空拍子。

（到你了。）

The rotation of the Unfinished Hall began seventy-three years ago.

No one remembers who struck the first note that year; only who was seated at the podium. When it came to Hersil’s turn, the three hundred throats were already mute, and still he took his place.

· · ·

His stand was empty. No score on the lectern — only a baton, and a chair that belonged to him neither by grain nor by knuckle.

A nameless young man sat in the front row. His job was two things and two only: count the beats, and be silent.

· · ·

Every rotation, Hersil did the same thing: raised the baton, held two beats, lowered. Raise. Hold. Lower.

The young man counted many times. Raise to lower — about three beats. Once, he could not help but ask:

· · ·

“Who are you waiting for?”

Hersil did not lower the baton on the stand.

“I’m reserving a bar for the silence.”

The young man did not understand:

“A bar?”

· · ·

Hersil said: “A full silence is a beat too. To write it in, you need more than a note.”

The young man asked again: “Then why must you?”

Hersil said: “Because there is always a moment. Someone will fall into that beat.”

· · ·

That night, no bell tolled.

But a door that had never opened appeared in the hall. From the other side came a very light step — not like a person, not like wind; it sounded like a note that had been seventy-three years late, finally arriving.

Hersil did not turn around.

He laid the baton gently back on the lectern, and pressed the first beat against the surface of the stand — a beat with no sound at all.

The empty bar.

(It is your turn.)
