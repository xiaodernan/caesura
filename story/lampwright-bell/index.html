灯匠

The Lampwright

风钟海岸最东端的灯坊

The lampforge at the eastern end of Windbell Coast

约 7 分钟 · 与 世界谱 同源世界观 · 零 LLM 撰写

about 7 minutes · same world as the codex · written without LLM assistance

风钟海岸最东端，有一间灯坊。这间灯坊从不关门——不是因为不关，而是因为没有什么可关的。阿瓷收下一批旧铜：钟的口、钟的舌、钟的碎片——八区的废品商贩廉价卖来。

她不是钟匠，不是铸师，她只是一个会把破铜熔进灯身的灯匠。但她不在乎敲打。她在乎的是：每一块铜都带着一圈完整的老痕迹。

· · ·

那个冬天，她每天关门后把铜片一片片排开。她发现它们并不都相同——有一块尤其重，几乎是其他的两倍；有一块很轻，几乎拍一下就响。她把它们排成一排，像是在替它们按音阶排队。

有一天夜里，一个行商敲开了灯坊的门——他不是来买灯的，是来卖铜的。他说这批旧铜来自八个区域：沉唱堂、叩钟镇、破拍营地——每一块都来自被碎掉的钟。每一块都被海蚀蚀。阿瓷点头，但她没有问来历——她不在乎来历。她在乎的是：这些铜自己记住了什么。

· · ·

议会的书记员每年来收税。他看到灯坊门口排着的铜片，皱眉说：这些铜都在发霉、会腐蚀、它们没用。阿瓷没有反驳。她只是说：这些铜不是用来用的。它们的的确确存在过。

书记员走后，阿瓷把那片最重的铜重新拿起来。那一刻铜片发出了很低的共鸣——像有人把他指尖深深按进烛芯，但还没按下去。她屏住了呼吸。

· · ·

那一冬之后的春天，一个老妇人来到灯坊。老妇人什么也没说，她只是把一只旧腕表递给阿瓷——那是一只从没走过的表。阿瓷打开它，里面的发条已完全锈死。她还给老妇人：这表再也走不动了。老妇人说：「我知道。」然后转身走了。

阿瓷那晚坐在灯坊里很久。她想到她手边排着的铜片——它们不也是一样的东西吗：从没响过，却从不真正沉默。她拿起那块最重的铜，用布擦了很久。铜片慢慢亮了起来——像有人把这块铜重新想起来了。

· · ·

那年夏末，海边卷来了一场风暴。渔村里有个孩子被卷入潮间带——灯坊的灯火忽然自己低了一扇。阿瓷没有犹豫。她从架子上取下那片最重的铜，用布裹住，冲了出去。

风暴到达岬角时，孩子还站在水里，腿脚冰凉。阿瓷把铜片举到孩子耳边，铜片自己震了一下——很轻，但足够让孩子闭上眼睛、抬头看天。然后铜片裂成了两半。

· · ·

孩子被找到了。阿瓷回灯坊，把铜片的两半放在架子上。那晚，整间灯坊都安静了。

她再也没有用那片铜做灯。她只是把那两半放在架子上，每天擦它一次。灯坊还是一样亮，铜片还是一样静。

被风蚀过的铜——
被打碎的钟仍在记忆里敲响；
被分两半的沉默依然等待一个整体的归来。

At the very eastern end of Windbell Coast, there is a lampforge. It never closes the door — not because it does not want to, but because there is nothing to close it for. A-Zi accepted a crate of old copper: bell-rims, bell-clappers, bell-shards — a scrap dealer bought them cheap from all eight reaches.

She is not a bell-smith, not a foundry-keeper. She is a lampwright who happens to be able to melt broken copper into lamp bodies. She does not care about striking. She cares about this: every piece of copper came with a full circle of old marks around it.

· · ·

That winter, she lined the pieces up every night after closing. She found they were not all the same — one piece was especially heavy, almost twice the weight of the others; one was almost light enough to bounce with a tap. She lined them up as though she were filing them by pitch.

One night, a traveling merchant knocked at the lampforge. He did not come to buy lamps — he came to sell more copper. He said this batch came from eight reaches: Drowned Choir, Fellcarillon, Brokenbar Camp — every piece came from a broken bell. Every piece had been etched by the sea. A-Zi nodded, but she did not ask where they came from. She did not care about provenance. She cared about what the copper itself remembered.

· · ·

The council clerk comes every year to collect taxes. He saw the copper pieces lined up at the door and frowned: these are all oxidizing, they will corrode, they are useless. A-Zi did not argue. She only said: these are not meant to be used. They were really here.

After the clerk left, A-Zi picked up the heaviest piece again. In that moment, the copper gave a very low resonance — as though someone had pressed a finger deep against a wick but had not yet pressed down. She held her breath.

· · ·

The following spring, an old woman came to the lampforge. She said nothing; she only handed A-Zi a broken old watch — a watch that had never run. A-Zi opened it: the mainspring was rusted completely dead. She gave it back. The old woman said: “I know.” Then she turned and left.

That night, A-Zi sat in the lampforge for a long time. She thought of the copper pieces lined up on her workbench — weren't they the same thing: never ringing, yet never truly silent? She picked up the heaviest piece and wiped it with a cloth for a very long time. The copper slowly began to shine — as though someone had just remembered this piece.

· · ·

At the end of that summer, a storm swept in from the sea. A child from the fishing village was swept into the tide-flats — and the lamp suddenly dimmed by one pane. A-Zi did not hesitate. She took the heaviest piece from the shelf, wrapped it in cloth, and ran out.

When the storm reached the headland, the child was still standing in the water, legs numb with cold. A-Zi held the copper piece near the child's ear; the copper chimed on its own — very softly, but enough to make the child close their eyes and look up at the sky. Then the copper piece cracked in two.

· · ·

The child was found. A-Zi returned to the lampforge and placed the two halves on the shelf. That night, the whole lampforge went quiet.

She never used the piece to make a lamp. She only kept the two halves on the shelf, wiping them once a day. The lampforge stayed as bright as ever, and the copper halves stayed as quiet as ever.

The wind will never forget —
the bells broken still ring inside the memory;
the silence split in two still waits for one whole return.
